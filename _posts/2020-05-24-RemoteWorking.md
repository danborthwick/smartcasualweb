The recent huge interest in remote working has been fascinating to follow. It seems to be widely agreed that the next few years will clearly be a period of major disruption to working patterns and norms for those in the service and information economies. As with any disruption there will be winners and losers, will Zoom or WebEx turn out to be the least crap method of trying to have a conversation while sat in bed wearing a shirt?

But personally I'm much more interested on how remote working will impact on the huge number of workers who might choose, or be chosen, to engage in it. The current popular discourse and media currently seems to present remote working as an almost exclusively positive opportunity for workers, with risks to mental health being one of the few areas for concern that I've seen, albeit a very important one.

But what about the potential for impact on workers' rights? History makes a compelling case that almost all of the rights that we currently take for granted have had to be fought for, and that when workers' power is weakened, for example in times of economic downturn, these rights can quickly be eroded. This post was triggered largely by [comments made by Mark Zuckerberg in his recent call to Facebook employees](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2020/may/21/facebook-coronavirus-remote-working-policy-extended-years) to "be the most forward-leaning company on remote work at our scale" and to create ‘hubs’ that "aren’t necessarily offices".

Yes there are some sucky things about offices, not least in traveling to them, but they also offer employees (and their employers) subtle tools that are so enmeshed in our daily work lives that we may not notice them until they’re no longer there. In sharing a space with our colleagues we also notice what others are doing, overhear conversations, participate in a shared social atmosphere, create opportunities to collaborate outside of the formal structures of the organisation. This is important not just for productivity and creativity, but also to control our own individual and collective work lives. Have you ever noticed when someone stopped being around so much? When a particular team stopped growing? When your normally bubbly manager started to seem unusually stressed? All of these could be signs of things happening outside of formal communication channels that we would want to know about, and provide opportunities to help ourselves and each other.

The answer we are told is in good leadership and better technology. But we all know that not all leaders are always good. That technology often restricts as much as it empowers. A world in which the only we can communicate with our peers through officially sanctioned, monitored tools with centrally controlled access offers a huge risk for misusez
